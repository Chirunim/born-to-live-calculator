Methodology & Sources

This page explains every step of the calculation and links to the data behind each figure. All source figures live in data/figures.json in the source repository.

Step 1 — Estimating your tax (T)

We calculate two components of your UK tax:

  • Income Tax — applied to your taxable income (gross salary minus personal allowance) at the rates for your region (rest-of-UK or Scotland).
  • Employee National Insurance (Class 1) — applied to earnings above the Primary Threshold at 8%, and above the Upper Earnings Limit at 2%.
T = Income Tax + National Insurance

Figures used:

  • Personal Allowance: £12,570 (tapers to £0 above £100,000) — gov.uk/income-tax-rates, 2024–25
  • rUK Income Tax bands: 20% / 40% / 45% — same source
  • Scottish Income Tax bands: 19% / 20% / 21% / 42% / 45% / 48% — gov.uk/scottish-income-tax, 2024–25
  • NI Primary Threshold: £12,570 · Upper Earnings Limit: £50,270 · rates: 8% / 2% — gov.uk/national-insurance-rates-letters, 2024–25

This is an annual approximation. NI is technically calculated per pay period; using annual salary slightly overstates NI for monthly-paid workers near the thresholds. The difference is small and does not affect the aid-chain result materially.

Step 2 — UK aid as a share of spending (a)

HMRC's Annual Tax Summary shows, for every pound collected, how much goes to each spending area. The "overseas aid" line gives us a — the fraction of Total Managed Expenditure that is Official Development Assistance (ODA).

a = UK ODA ÷ Total Managed Expenditure

Step 3 — Health aid as a share of UK ODA (h)

Not all UK aid goes to health. The FCDO Statistics on International Development breaks bilateral ODA by sector; we take the health sector total as a fraction of gross bilateral ODA.

h = bilateral health ODA ÷ total bilateral ODA
  • Source: FCDO — Statistics on International Development
  • Value: TODO — pending manual verification

Step 4 — Maternal, newborn and child health share (c)

Within health aid, we focus on the sub-sectors most relevant to children: maternal and newborn care, reproductive health, nutrition, and basic primary care. We identify these using OECD Creditor Reporting System (CRS) purpose codes:

  • 13010 — Population policy and reproductive health
  • 13020 — Reproductive health care
  • 13030 — Family planning
  • 12191 — Medical services (nutrition)
  • 12220 — Basic health care
  • 12261 — Health education
c = UK disbursements to above codes ÷ total UK health ODA
  • Source: OECD — Creditor Reporting System
  • Value (central / low / high): TODO — pending manual verification
  • Low and high are taken from the range of annual values over the most recent three to five years, to reflect genuine year-to-year variation in aid priorities.

Step 5 — Your contribution to child health (C)

Combining steps 1–4:

C = T × a × h × c

C is the estimated amount of your tax that reaches maternal, newborn and child health programmes through UK official development assistance, in a given year.

We also show a per-day figure: C ÷ 365.

Step 6 — What C buys (unit costs)

We convert C into three tangible items using published procurement prices. All costs are in USD and converted to GBP at the Bank of England 2024 annual average rate (1 USD = £0.7874, source: Bank of England).

  • Pentavalent vaccine dose (DTP-HepB-Hib): $0.75–$2.50 per dose — UNICEF Supply Division vaccine price data, 2024
  • Bednet (LLIN) distributed: $3.50–$6.00 per net including logistics — Against Malaria Foundation distribution data, 2023
  • ORS treatment course (one diarrhoea episode, ~2–3 sachets): $0.30–$0.80 per course — UNICEF Supply Division essential medicines catalogue, 2024

Step 7 — Monte Carlo uncertainty

We are not certain of the exact values of c (the MNCH share of health aid) or the precise unit costs — both vary by year and country. To show this uncertainty honestly, we run 10,000 simulations, each time drawing c and each unit cost independently from a uniform distribution between its low and high values.

The results shown are the 10th–90th percentile range across those 10,000 draws, with the median (~50th percentile) shown as the typical estimate.

This means: in 80% of plausible scenarios, the true figure lies within the shown range.

What this estimate does not include

  • Multilateral aid contributions (e.g. via GAVI, UNICEF, World Bank) — these flow through organisations rather than directly from FCDO bilateral spend and are harder to attribute.
  • Non-monetary UK contributions (technical assistance, policy influence).
  • Tax paid outside Income Tax and NI (e.g. VAT, capital gains) — methodologically complex and beyond the HMRC Annual Tax Summary methodology we follow.
